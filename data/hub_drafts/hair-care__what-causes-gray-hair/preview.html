What Causes Gray Hair? Every Cause, Explained by the Research

Gray (or grey) hair happens when the pigment cells inside your hair follicles, called melanocytes, slow down or stop making melanin. Genetics sets the timing for most people. Age, stress, smoking and some nutrient shortfalls can move it earlier or speed it up.

This guide explains each cause, when graying usually starts, what you can and can't change, and when it's worth seeing a doctor. Each cause links to a deeper guide.

Key fact: Every hair color change happens at the root, inside the follicle, while the hair is growing. A strand that has already grown out of your scalp can't change color on its own, whatever stress or shock it goes through.

The Short Answer

  • Hair turns gray when melanocytes in the follicle make less melanin, or stop making it.
  • Genetics and age are the biggest factors. Stress, smoking, low vitamin B12, folate or iron stores, and some medical conditions are linked to graying earlier.
  • Most people see some gray by middle age, but the "50% gray by 50" rule is a myth. The real figure is far lower.
  • Some causes you can't change. Others, like smoking, chronic stress and nutrient gaps, are worth addressing.

What Is Gray Hair, Really?

Gray hair is hair that grows in with little or no melanin. Each follicle has its own pigment cells. While they keep working, new hair grows in with color. When they slow down, new hair grows in lighter. When they stop, it grows in white, and white hairs mixed with colored ones look gray.

The medical term for graying is canities. When it starts unusually early for a person's background, it's called premature canities, or premature graying.

Genetics

Genetics is the biggest single factor. If your parents went gray early, you're more likely to. Genes set a tendency, not a date, which is why siblings can still gray at different rates. How hair pigment works: the melanocyte story.

Age and Stem-Cell Decline

Each new hair draws on a small reserve of melanocyte stem cells that replace worn-out pigment cells. That reserve shrinks with age. Once it runs low, the follicle can't keep making colored hair, which is the main driver of ordinary age-related graying.

Oxidative Stress and Hydrogen Peroxide

Making pigment produces reactive molecules, including hydrogen peroxide. Normally the follicle's antioxidant defenses keep them in check. When those defenses weaken with age, oxidative damage builds up and pigment production falls. More on hydrogen peroxide and gray hair, and the role of catalase.

At What Age Does Hair Normally Turn Gray?

Most people have at least some gray hair by middle age, but far fewer are "half gray by 50" than the old rule of thumb claims. Timing depends heavily on ancestry, and men tend to gray more than women.

A worldwide survey of 4,192 people (Panhard et al., 2012) found that 74% of people aged 45 to 65 had some gray hair, covering about 27% of their hair on average. Only 6% to 23% had at least half gray hair at age 50, depending on ancestry and natural hair color. People of Asian and African descent had less gray than people of Caucasian descent at the same age.

Graying that starts much earlier than usual for your background is called premature graying. Our guide to premature and early graying covers the age cut-offs and causes.

What Does the Research Say About Each Risk Factor?

A 2020 systematic review of premature graying (Mahendiratta et al.) pulled together the evidence on risk factors. The ones most consistently associated with graying early were smoking and low levels of vitamin B12, folic acid and biotin (B7), plus low calcium and low ferritin, the body's iron store.

Other reported risk factors included family history, obesity, high blood pressure, lack of exercise, some drugs, genetic syndromes, high cholesterol, thyroid disorders and changes in liver function. The review also found that treating the underlying condition led to color returning in many of the cases studied.

Stress has its own line of evidence. A 2021 study (Rosenberg et al.) mapped pigment along individual human hairs and found gray hairs that naturally regained color, across sexes, ethnicities, ages and body sites. Some greying and regaining of color happened in parallel with periods of psychological stress. The researchers suggest graying may be temporarily reversible up to a threshold.

Can Gray Hair Be Reversed?

Sometimes, partly, and not on demand. The research above shows some gray hairs can regain color naturally, and that treating an underlying cause can help in some cases. No supplement, serum or treatment has been shown to reliably restore full color. Can gray hair be reversed? The full answer.

Can You Prevent or Slow Gray Hair?

You can't change your genes, but you can work on the factors linked to graying earlier:

  • Don't smoke, or quit if you do.
  • Check your nutrient levels. Ask your doctor about B12, folate and iron (ferritin), and correct any deficiency that's confirmed.
  • Manage chronic stress, with sleep, exercise and cutting ongoing stressors where you can. Practical ways to lower everyday stress.
  • Eat for antioxidant support. A diet rich in fruit, vegetables and protein supports the body's own defenses. Foods that may slow premature graying.
  • Be patient. Hair grows slowly, so judge any change over at least three months.

When Gray Hair Might Signal Something Else

For most people, gray hair is just a normal part of aging. Talk to a doctor if graying is sudden, patchy or starts unusually early, or comes with fatigue, skin changes, weight changes or other symptoms.

Common Myths

Plucking a gray hair doesn't make more grow back. Stress can't turn a hair gray overnight, because a grown strand can't change color. Dyeing your hair doesn't speed up graying, because dye works on the strand, not the follicle. Gray hair myths vs. facts.

Frequently Asked Questions

What causes gray hair?

Gray hair happens when the pigment cells in the hair follicle make less melanin or stop making it. Genetics and age are the main drivers. Stress, smoking, low B12, folate or iron stores, and some medical conditions are linked to graying earlier.

What is the average age to get gray hair?

Most people have some gray by middle age, and timing varies a lot by ancestry. In a worldwide survey, 74% of people aged 45 to 65 had some gray hair, but only 6% to 23% were at least half gray at 50.

Is gray hair caused by stress?

Stress is linked to graying, but it works slowly, at the root, while a hair grows. Research on individual hairs found graying and some return of color that tracked periods of stress.

Why am I suddenly getting a lot of gray hair?

A faster pace of graying can reflect genetics catching up, a stressful period, smoking or a nutrient shortfall. If it's sudden, patchy or comes with other symptoms, see a doctor to rule out a medical cause.

What vitamin deficiency causes gray hair?

Low vitamin B12, folic acid and biotin, and low ferritin (iron stores) and calcium, have been linked to premature graying. Only correct a deficiency that a blood test confirms.

How do I stop my hair from graying?

You can't stop genetic graying, but you can address the factors linked to graying early: don't smoke, manage stress, and correct confirmed nutrient deficiencies.

Is going gray purely genetic?

No. Genetics sets the baseline, but smoking, stress, nutrient levels and some health conditions also play a part.
