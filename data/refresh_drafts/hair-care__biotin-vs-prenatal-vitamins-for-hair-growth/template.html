Biotin is a B vitamin that hair really does depend on: a true biotin deficiency causes hair loss. Prenatal vitamins are built for pregnancy, and there's no good evidence that either one grows hair in people who aren't short on something.

Here's what the research says about each, and who might need which.

Key Takeaways

  • Biotin deficiency causes hair loss and a scaly rash, and supplementing helps when deficiency or a related condition is the cause.
  • For people who aren't deficient, a review of the three studies of oral biotin for hair found that the best one (double-blind and placebo-controlled) showed no difference from placebo.
  • Prenatal vitamins are designed around the needs of pregnancy, not hair. We found no study testing them for hair growth.
  • If you're losing hair, finding the cause matters more than choosing between them.

The Quick Answer

  • If you're pregnant or planning to be: follow your doctor's advice on a prenatal vitamin. It's chosen for pregnancy, not hair.
  • If you're not pregnant and want hair support: neither has strong evidence. Biotin has a role if you're deficient, and we found no hair research on prenatal vitamins.
  • If you're losing hair: see a doctor who can take a history, examine you and order targeted tests, rather than guessing at supplements.

What About Prenatal Vitamins?

Prenatal vitamins are formulated to meet the needs of pregnancy. They contain a mix of nutrients, and more of a nutrient isn't automatically better, so ask your doctor before taking one if you aren't pregnant.

We found no study testing prenatal vitamins for hair growth in people who aren't deficient. If hair is the goal, the more useful step is to find out whether you're short on anything.

Who Should Choose Which?

  • You have a diagnosed biotin deficiency, or a doctor has recommended biotin: biotin is the one with a clear role.
  • You're pregnant or planning to be: ask your doctor about a prenatal vitamin.
  • You're just trying to improve thinning hair: get tested first. Ask your doctor about iron, vitamin D and other markers rather than guessing.

What Does Biotin Do For Hair?

Biotin is a water-soluble vitamin that acts as a cofactor for four carboxylase enzymes. In infants, children and adults, deficiency causes alopecia and a characteristic scaly, red rash around body openings (Mock, 1991).

In a review of 18 reported cases of biotin use for hair and nail changes, every patient had an underlying cause for poor hair or nail growth, and all improved after biotin. The reviewers concluded that biotin may help in acquired or inherited biotin deficiency and in conditions like brittle nails or uncombable hair, but that these cases are uncommon and there is a lack of sufficient evidence for supplementation in healthy people (Patel et al., 2017).

A later review found only three studies of oral biotin for hair growth or quality. The best, a double-blind placebo-controlled study, found no difference between biotin and placebo. The other two were in specific groups (patients on isotretinoin and women after sleeve gastrectomy) and had a risk of bias (Yelich et al., 2024).

Is It Safe?

Stick to the label amounts of any supplement, and don't combine several products that contain the same nutrient. Tell your doctor which supplements you take, especially before blood tests. Talk to your doctor if you're pregnant, breastfeeding, have a condition or take medication.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is biotin or prenatal vitamins better for hair growth?

Neither has strong evidence in people who aren't deficient. Biotin matters when you're deficient, and the best study of oral biotin for hair found no difference from placebo. We found no hair studies of prenatal vitamins.

Can I take prenatal vitamins if I'm not pregnant?

They're formulated for pregnancy, so ask your doctor first. More of a nutrient isn't automatically better.

How do I know if I'm short on a nutrient?

Ask your doctor about testing rather than guessing. Hair loss has many causes, and a diagnosis comes first.
