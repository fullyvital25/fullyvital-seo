Brushing your hair is not the same as the scalp massage that has been studied. A small study of a standardized scalp massage found increased hair thickness after 24 weeks, but we found no study that brushing 100 or 200 times a day grows hair, and one study of Afro hair found that damage rose with the number of combing strokes.

Here's what the research says about scalp massage, what it suggests about brushing, and how to be gentle with your hair.

Does Brushing Your Scalp Make Hair Grow?

The idea comes from the old advice to brush 100 times a day. The research doesn't test that. What it tests is massage, which applies pressure and stretching to the scalp, and the one thing it measured was hair thickness.

Brushing, Massage And Shedding

Brushing a lot isn't the same as massaging. Brushing and combing put repeated mechanical stress on hair fibers, while a massage moves the scalp skin with your fingers or a device. If you're losing hair, see a dermatologist rather than relying on either.

How To Be Gentle

  • Use a brush that glides through your hair without pulling.
  • Don't count strokes. More isn't better for hair fibers.

What The Research Shows

A Standardized Scalp Massage

Nine healthy men received 4 minutes of standardized scalp massage a day for 24 weeks using a device. Hair thickness increased after 24 weeks (0.085 vs 0.092 mm). In the lab, stretching human dermal papilla cells changed the expression of thousands of genes, including hair-cycle genes. The authors concluded that massage transmits mechanical stress to dermal papilla cells (Koyama et al., 2016). It was a very small study.

A Survey Of People With Pattern Hair Loss

In a retrospective survey of 1,899 people invited and 340 who responded, 327 tried a twice-daily, 20-minute scalp massage routine. 68.9% reported hair loss stabilization or regrowth, and more time spent massaging was linked to more perceived change. It was self-reported and had no control group (English & Barazesh, 2019).

Combing Strokes And Damage

In 30 women, frequent braiders had thinner, weaker hair with more cuticle damage and higher traction alopecia scores than occasional braiders. In a separate test on naturally curly Afro hair, surface fibre damage rose linearly with the number of combing strokes, and cuticle damage from 480 strokes was more severe than from frequent braiding (Molamodi et al., 2021). The study was on Afro hair.
