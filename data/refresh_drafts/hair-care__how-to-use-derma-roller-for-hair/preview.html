How to Use a Derma Roller for Hair: What the Research Says About Technique & Safety

There's no study-backed step-by-step for using a derma roller at home. The research is on microneedling done in clinics, mostly alongside minoxidil, using needle depths from 0.5 to 2.5 mm, and a published case report shows what can go wrong with home use.

Here's what the research says about technique, benefits and safety, and what to ask a dermatologist before trying it.

Key Takeaways

  • A review of 22 clinical studies found microneedling improved hair parameters as an adjunct, using needle depths from 0.50 to 2.50 mm and session frequencies from weekly to monthly.
  • The review rated the data as relatively low quality.
  • A systematic review found microneedling is relatively safe, with expected redness, pain, swelling and temporary irritation.
  • A case report describes a serious scalp infection after home derma rolling.

What Is A Derma Roller?

A derma roller is a handheld device with fine needles. Rolling it over the scalp makes tiny punctures in the skin, which is the principle behind microneedling.

Can You Do It At Home?

The studies used medical-grade devices, and none of the abstracts we read give a home protocol. The safest first step is to ask a dermatologist whether microneedling is right for you and how to do it.

What To Ask Your Dermatologist

  • Needle depth and frequency. Studies used a range of depths and schedules.
  • Hygiene and infection risk. Microneedling breaks the skin.
  • What to apply afterwards. Don't guess.
  • Whether it suits your hair loss. The evidence is mostly for androgenetic alopecia.

What The Research Shows

Depth, Frequency And Results

A review of 22 clinical studies (1,127 subjects) found microneedling improved hair parameters as an adjunct across types and severities of hair loss, with needle depths from 0.50 to 2.50 mm and session frequencies from once weekly to monthly. It helped in androgenic alopecia when paired with 5% minoxidil, growth factor solutions or PRP. No serious adverse events were reported among 657 subjects, and the reviewers called the data low quality and recommended large randomized trials (English et al., 2022).

Safety

A systematic review of 51 articles (1,029 patients) found microneedling to be relatively safe with minimal adverse effects, including erythema, pain, edema and temporary skin irritation, and noted increasing use at home as well as in clinics (Gowda et al., 2021).

A Home-Use Case Report

A 28-year-old man treating alopecia at home with minoxidil spray followed by derma roller microneedling developed a subpericranial and epidural abscess with skull osteomyelitis, and surgery to evacuate it was planned. The report concluded that scalp hematomas can become infected after procedures like microneedling without an open wound (Jain et al., 2026). This was a single case.
