Pine Bark Extract for Hair: What the Research Shows

Pine bark extract has one small randomized trial on hair: Chinese menopausal women taking French maritime pine bark extract (sold as Pycnogenol) had higher hair density than those taking placebo. That is a single study, in one population, of one branded extract.

Here's what it found, and what the wider pine bark research says.

Key Takeaways

  • One double-blind trial in 76 menopausal women found hair density rose 30% at 2 months and 23% at 6 months with a pine bark extract, and not with placebo.
  • A trial in 112 women with photoaged facial skin found improved skin scores with 40 mg or 100 mg a day, which was a skin study, not a hair study.
  • A Cochrane review of 27 trials across ten chronic disorders concluded that no definitive conclusions about efficacy or safety are possible.

What Is Pine Bark Extract?

Pine bark extract is a plant extract rich in proanthocyanidins, a type of antioxidant. The best-known form comes from French maritime pine.

Does Pine Bark Help Hair?

Possibly in one group of women, based on a single trial. It hasn't been tested widely, so we can't say who it helps.

What The Research Shows

Hair Density In Menopausal Women

In a single-center, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled study, 76 Han Chinese menopausal women in Shanghai took Pycnogenol 3 x 50 mg a day for 6 months. Hair density, measured by digital photographs and Trichoscan, rose 30% after 2 months and 23% after 6 months, and not in the placebo group. Scalp resting flux decreased, which the authors said might indicate improved microcirculation. They concluded it may have the potential to reduce hair loss in postmenopausal women (Cai et al., 2023).

Skin, Not Hair

In a randomized trial of 112 women with mild to moderate photoaging, 100 mg a day or 40 mg a day of French maritime pine bark extract was linked to lower photoaging scores and less pigmentation of age spots over 12 weeks. It measured facial skin, not hair (Furumura et al., 2012).

The Wider Evidence

A Cochrane review included 27 randomized trials with 1,641 participants across ten chronic disorders. The authors concluded that small sample sizes, few trials per condition, varied outcome measures and poor reporting mean no definitive conclusions about the efficacy or safety of pine bark extract supplements are possible. For most comparisons, studies did not report serious adverse events (Robertson et al., 2020). Hair loss was not among the conditions reviewed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does pine bark help hair growth?

One trial in menopausal women found higher hair density than placebo. More research is needed.

How long does it take to see results?

The trial measured at 2 and 6 months.

Is pine bark extract safe?

A Cochrane review said no definitive conclusions about safety are possible. Talk to your doctor.
