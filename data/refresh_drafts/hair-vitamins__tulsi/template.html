Tulsi, also called holy basil, is a herb with a long traditional history, and it is often linked to hair through stress. We found no study of tulsi on hair. The human research is on stress, mood and other outcomes, so any link to hair is an inference, not a finding.

Key Takeaways

  • We found no human study of tulsi for hair growth or hair loss.
  • A systematic review of 24 human studies reported favourable outcomes for metabolic, cardiovascular, immune and neurocognitive measures, with no significant adverse events reported.
  • In a placebo-controlled trial of 100 adults under stress, a holy basil extract was linked to lower perceived stress scores and lower hair cortisol after 8 weeks.
  • A systematic review found that, for most plants tested, the effect on the stress hormone system in humans is unclear.

What Is Tulsi?

Tulsi (Ocimum tenuiflorum, also called Ocimum sanctum) is a basil native to the Indian subcontinent, long used in Ayurveda.

What The Research Shows

Human Studies Overall

A systematic review of human research on tulsi identified 24 studies reporting therapeutic effects on metabolic disorders, cardiovascular disease, immunity and neurocognition. All reported favourable clinical outcomes, and none reported significant adverse events. The authors said further studies are required to clarify dosage, dose form and the populations most likely to benefit (Jamshidi and Cohen, 2017). Hair was not among the outcomes.

A Stress Trial

In an 8-week randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, 100 adults aged 18 to 65 who were experiencing stress took 125 mg of a holy basil extract twice daily or placebo. Compared with placebo, the extract was linked to greater improvements in perceived stress and sleep scores, and lower hair cortisol at week 8. It was well tolerated, and the authors called for further research on the sleep findings (Lopresti et al., 2022). Hair here was used only to measure cortisol, not hair growth.

How Clear Is The Stress Effect?

A systematic review of 52 human trials of single plants and their effect on stress-axis hormones included holy basil. Because of large differences between studies, the effect of plant products on this system in humans was unclear, and the most consistent finding was for ashwagandha (Lopresti et al., 2022).

Frequently Asked Questions

Does tulsi help hair growth?

We found no study showing that.

Can tulsi reduce stress?

One placebo-controlled trial found lower stress scores, but a wider review found the evidence unclear for most plants.

Is tulsi safe?

A systematic review found no significant adverse events reported in 24 human studies. Talk to your doctor first.
