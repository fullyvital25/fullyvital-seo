Key Takeaways

  • Vitamin A is necessary for the growth of healthy skin and hair, but both too little and too much have harmful effects.
  • Retinoic acid, its active metabolite, regulates hair follicle stem cells in a dose-dependent way.
  • A systematic review of toxic agents that cause alopecia lists vitamin A among those with described incidents.
  • Reviews say micronutrient deficiencies may be modifiable risk factors for hair loss, but large trials are needed.

What Is Vitamin A?

Vitamin A is a fat-soluble micronutrient. Its main active metabolites are retinoic acid and retinal.

Does More Vitamin A Mean More Hair?

No. The relationship is dose-dependent and can turn harmful at high intakes. If you're worried about hair loss, a doctor can check whether you're short of anything rather than you adding vitamin A on a guess.

What The Research Shows

Vitamin A In Skin And Hair

A review describes vitamin A as necessary for the growth of healthy skin and hair, with both too little and too much deleterious. Retinoic acid dose-dependently regulates hair follicle stem cells, influencing the hair cycle, wound healing and melanocyte stem cells (VanBuren & Everts, 2022).

When Vitamin A Is Too Much

A systematic review of 47 studies on toxic agents that cause alopecia found the strongest evidence for thallium, mercury, selenium and colchicine. Agents with described incidents include boric acid, arsenic, vitamin A, botulinum toxin and others (Yu et al., 2018).

The Wider Micronutrient Picture

A review of vitamins and minerals in hair loss, including vitamins A, B, C, D, E, iron, selenium and zinc, found they play important but not entirely clear roles in hair follicle development and immune function. It said deficiency may be a modifiable risk factor and that large double-blind placebo-controlled trials are required (Almohanna et al., 2019).
